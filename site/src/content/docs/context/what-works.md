Eight policy areas are open and every one of them was opened by something going wrong. That is how
[the pipeline](../pipeline.md) works, and it has a predictable failure mode: a platform built
entirely from grievances ends up describing a city nobody would choose to live in, and then
proposes to govern it. The person giving these accounts has lived here by choice for ten years.

So this page records the other half of the account, in the same voice and to the same standard —
which is to say, unverified.

## The account

Jerusalem is a slower pace of life than Tel Aviv. And for all the challenges, there is a certain
easy familiarity about the city. You run into people you know.

The market is really nice.

It is the central point of Judaism and of Jewish thought, and there are some genuinely great
institutions here: government, think tanks, policy. Potentially — at least sometimes, during good
times — it is a very livable city.

I came from a city called Cork in Ireland, the smaller of the two, with much the same dynamic that
Jerusalem has with Tel Aviv. I recognise the shape of it.

## The part that is a strategy argument

I think there is great potential in Jerusalem for remote work, given that we now have good fibre
penetration. And I think the Tel Aviv versus Jerusalem outlook is outdated: fast, reliable trains
connect Jerusalem to Tel Aviv and to the rest of the centre, so Jerusalem is not the distant,
out-of-the-way backwater it may once have been thought. It is very connected to government and very
connected to Tel Aviv.

A lot of people seem to be hitching their plans for Jerusalem to the back of a wave of diplomatic
events — the opening of embassies. That couples Jerusalem's destiny, its vitality and its capacity
to political developments, and I think that is a strategic mistake.

## Why this page exists, and what it is not

It is not balance for its own sake, and it is not a disclaimer attached to the criticism. It does
two things the policy areas cannot.

**It names what a proposal must not break.** The pace, the familiarity, the scale at which you run
into people you know — those are the things a building programme of the kind described in
[housing supply](../policy/housing-supply/index.md) and
[construction disruption](../policy/construction-disruption/index.md) puts at risk, and a platform
that only counts the costs of the works will not notice when a proposal trades one of them away.

**It supplies the alternative the criticism implies.** The complaint in
[capital priorities](../policy/capital-priorities/index.md) is that the city banks on tourism,
which is volatile and undiversified. The same objection is made here to banking on diplomatic
events. Both are exogenous — the city does not control either, and neither responds to anything a
mayor does. Remote work, fibre and rail connectivity are offered as the diversified alternative:
things that serve residents whether or not the embassies open.

That symmetry is the useful part. "Do not bank the city's future on events it does not control" is
one argument appearing twice, and it is the closest thing in this material to a positive economic
thesis. It is also completely unverified.
