Opened by [An hour to Talpiot, four hours home](../../testimonies/2026-08-10-public-transport.md),
10 August 2026, and extended later the same day by
[roving gangs of inspectors](../../testimonies/2026-08-10-public-transport-enforcement.md). The
area was opened as `buses` and renamed when the second account arrived: it is about the light rail
as much as the buses.

**The thesis, provisionally.** This is the area where
[the powers doctrine](../../method/powers-doctrine.md) is most likely to earn its keep, because the
obvious attribution is almost certainly wrong. Bus services in Israel are franchised and specified
by the Ministry of Transport, and run by contracted operators. A mayor does not set a timetable.

So the honest question is not "will you restore the frequency" — a promise no mayor can keep — but
**what does the municipality actually hold inside a nationally franchised service?** The candidates
are specific and unglamorous, which by this project's standards makes them promising: bus lanes and
signal priority on municipal roads, the physical stop and its shelter and shade, the information
displayed at that stop, the diversion notices when its own works move a route, and the city's
standing as the loudest possible advocate to the ministry.

The shade point is worth flagging early. The testimony's "it was extremely hot" is not colour — a
bus stop with no shelter in a Jerusalem summer is a different service from one with shelter, and
street furniture is the kind of thing a municipality usually does own outright. If it turns out the
city can shelter and shade every stop on a diverted route and has not, that is a class-A finding
sitting in plain sight.

**The enforcement half is the harder half, and probably not ours.** Fares, fare policy and
inspection powers sit with the national transport regulator and the operators holding the
concessions, so a mayor promising to call off the inspectors is very likely promising something
that is not theirs to call off. That expected **D** is worth publishing rather than burying: it is
precisely the kind of claim a campaign makes and cannot deliver.

Two things survive that finding, though, and they are what the research should chase. The city
sits inside the light rail's governance in some form, and whatever that form is, it is a seat at a
table where inspector conduct could be raised. And the account contains a sharp, checkable irony
rather than only a complaint: a city that has bet its capital on tourism —
[capital priorities](../capital-priorities/index.md) — is handing tourists their first impression
of the country through a fare inspector. If the municipality has any voice on how the service
treats visitors, that argument is available to it whether or not the enforcement power is.

Nothing below stage 1 has been done.

## Where this connects

The testimony is one journey, and it crosses four of the eight open areas: construction everywhere
on the way out, buses on the way back, shops gone in Talpiot, and towers going up where they were.
The account draws the connection itself — *there needs to be more housing, but the transition is
coming at a huge cost for residents* — and that sentence is closer to a platform thesis than
anything else captured so far.

It is not a finding. It is the hypothesis the eight areas are collectively testing: not whether the
building programme is wrong, but whether the city has made any serious attempt to carry residents
through it. That framing sits alongside
[the argument the city is having](../../context/the-ideological-agenda.md).

### Attributions to test

| # | The account assumes | To test |
|:-:|---|---|
| 1 | The municipality is responsible for bus frequency | Almost certainly not. Franchising and specification are national. Expect **D** on the timetable itself — and the point of saying so is to stop a mayoral platform promising something it cannot deliver |
| 2 | The municipality is responsible for the diversion and for explaining it | Splits. If the city's own works closed the road, the diversion is downstream of a municipal decision even where the reroute is the operator's. Expect **A** on notification, and that asymmetry is the whole area |
| 3 | Somebody should have made the diversions legible | A duty has to exist before its breach means anything. If none does, the finding is that the standard is too low rather than that it was missed — the same shape as [construction disruption](../construction-disruption/index.md) |
| 4 | Waiting in that heat is somebody's problem | Test stop and shelter ownership first. If shelters are municipal, this is **A** and cheap. If they sit inside an advertising concession, the lever is the next tender |
| 5 | The municipality is "creating and driving all of this" | The framing charge of the account, and its most testable claim. The building programme, the light rail and the bus network have at least three different holders between them. Expect the answer to be "partly", and expect the parts to matter |
| 6 | The city could have provided alternatives during the transition | The proposal-shaped question. Whatever the answer on frequency, mitigation — shuttles, shade, information, temporary stops — is much more likely to be municipal than the service itself |
| 7 | The enforcement regime is something the city could call off | Expect **D**. Fares and inspection powers are national and contractual. Publishing that plainly is more useful than a promise, because this is exactly the pledge a campaign would make and fail to keep |
| 8 | "We have made the experience almost as unpleasant as possible" | The "we" is doing a lot of work. Establish who each decision belongs to before accepting a collective municipal subject — it may turn out no municipal actor chose any of it |
| 9 | Deregulation caused this | Probably a mis-description of competitive tendering. If so it is a wrong-attribution finding of the ordinary kind: the reform's actual shape determines who can change the outcome |
| 10 | Overcrowding is a municipal failure | Capacity is a specification question and specification is national. Expect **C** or **D**, with any municipal lever running through bus priority on its own roads rather than through fleet size |
| 11 | Fare-free or low-enforcement transit is the better model | The account's proposed direction, and it gets the same scrutiny as the criticism. Establish who could fund it before it is described as available |
