My wife is an architect, so I see this from closer than most renters do.

Jerusalem is, by and large, a poor city. It is consistently ranked the poorest city in Israel.
And yet the municipality is fixated on building a vast stock of high-rise construction, built
explicitly as luxury and marketed to the Jewish community broadly.

Often these are investment properties outright. Even where they are not marketed as investment
properties, they play — rather ironically — into the idea of the diaspora as an unending pool of
wealth, where everyone can simply afford a huge luxury condominium in Jerusalem.

So you have a housing crisis, and the answer being built is not addressed to the people living
through it. The stock going up is not stock for the city's own income profile.

I am aware that this is an observation about what I watch being built and marketed, rather than
an account of something that happened to my household. The thing that happened to my household
is [the renting](./2026-08-10-renting.md), and the two are the same problem seen from two ends:
what is available to rent, and at what price, is downstream of what gets built and who it is
built for.
